Warum Polnischsprachige Vorteile haben

You already know more German than you think

Polish and German have been neighbours for a thousand years. Polish already has three genders, cases (German has four, you have seven), the same final-consonant rule (Hund = „hunt", like chleb = „chlep") and hundreds of German loanwords: dach, ratusz, urlop, kelner, szukać. Tap any to hear the German.

1 · Aussprache · wymowa

German sounds, written the Polish way

Many German spellings are Polish sounds in disguise: German sch = sz, tsch = cz, z = c, w = w. Read the right-hand column as Polish and you will be very close.

GermanSounds likeExampleRead it as
Achtung!German ie is a long i, not „je": Liebe = „liibe", vier = „fiir". And German ei = aj (mein = „majn"), eu = oj (heute = „hojte"). The opposite of what Polish eyes expect!
Long vowelsPolish has no long vowels; German does, and they change the meaning: Staat (state) vs. Stadt (city), Miete (rent) vs. Mitte (middle). A double vowel, ie or h means long. And two new vowels: ü (say i with rounded lips) and ö (say e with rounded lips).
StressPolish stresses the second-to-last syllable. German usually stresses the first syllable of the stem: AR-bei-ten, FAH-ren, but be-SU-chen (be- is never stressed).
2 · Satzbau · szyk zdania

Polish: free order. German: the verb is always 2nd

Thanks to cases, Polish word order is free. German is strict: in a main clause the conjugated verb is in position 2, whatever comes first. A second verb, a participle or a separable prefix goes to the end.

Ich arbeite heute.Pracuję dzisiaj.
Heute arbeite ich.Time first? The verb stays in position 2 and the subject moves behind it. Never „Heute ich arbeite".
Ich muss heute arbeiten.Muszę dziś pracować. (the infinitive goes to the end)
Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf.Wstaję o 7. The prefix auf- jumps to the end.
Kommen Sie aus Polen?Czy jest Pan / Pani z Polski? German has no „czy": the verb comes first.
Always a subjectPolish drops the subject: pracuję = I work. German never does: ich arbeite, es regnet (pada), es ist kalt (jest zimno).
Sie = Pan / PaniGerman has one word for Pan, Pani and Państwo: Sie, always with a capital S and the "they" verb: Sie sind (not „Sie ist"). ihr = wy: ihr seid = wy jesteście.
3 · Artikel & Genus · rodzaj

Three genders, like Polish, but German shows them with an article

Polish has no articles: the ending shows the gender (dom, kobieta, okno). German puts der, die or das in front. Often the gender matches, but not always.

Two articlesGerman also has ein / eine (a, one) and der / die / das (the). Polish has neither, so you will forget them at first. Rule of thumb: first mention = ein, already known = der. -ung, -heit, -keit = always die, -chen = always das.

Teil 1 · Formular

Fill in the form for your friend

Ihr Freund Tomasz Kowalski ist 32 Jahre alt und kommt aus Breslau in Polen. Er wohnt jetzt in der Königstraße 12, 70173 Stuttgart. Er ist Elektriker von Beruf. Er arbeitet tagsüber und möchte einen Deutschkurs am Abend machen.

Tomasz wants to register at the Volkshochschule. Write the 5 missing details. Twój kolega Tomasz chce zapisać się na kurs niemieckiego. Wpisz 5 brakujących informacji.

Achtung!German forms use Vorname (imię) and Familienname / Nachname (nazwisko). The postcode (PLZ) comes before the city: 70173 Stuttgart. Country: Polen, with no article. Germans often use German city names: Breslau = Wrocław, Danzig = Gdańsk, Krakau = Kraków.

Teil 1 · buchstabieren

Spell your name the German way

You will be asked to spell your name. Orange letters are traps for Polish speakers: E is a long „e", V is „fau", W is „we" (not wu), Y is „üpsilon" (not igrek), Z is „cet" (not zet). Good news: C = „ce", H = „ha" and J = „jot", like Polish. Tap a letter to hear it.
