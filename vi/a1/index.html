Was Vietnamesischsprachige schon können

Your head start: the Latin alphabet

You already read the Latin alphabet, so German letters look familiar: only ä, ö, ü and ß are new. German has no tones: the melody of a word never changes its meaning. Instead, German has things Vietnamese doesn't: verb endings, articles (der, die, das), plural endings and cases. The good news: the basic word order (I – work – today) is the same as in Vietnamese. Tap any to hear the German.

1 · Aussprache · phát âm

German sounds, written the Vietnamese way

Read the right-hand column as Vietnamese, without tones. German stresses the first syllable of most words: Ar-beit, Fa-mi-lie is an exception (Fa-mi-lie).

Achtung!Say the last consonant. In Vietnamese, final sounds are short and unreleased (học, mát). In German you must hear them: Hund = „hun-t", Berg = „béc-k". Many words end in two or three consonants: Herbst, Obst, Wurst. Say every one.
No tonesGerman uses melody only for feeling and for questions: the voice goes up at the end of a yes/no question. Don't worry about „wrong tones": a German word means the same at any pitch.
2 · Satzbau · trật tự câu

Same start as Vietnamese, but the verb is always 2nd

Vietnamese and German both say Ich arbeite heute = Tôi làm việc hôm nay. But in German the conjugated verb must stay in position 2, and a second verb goes to the end.

Ich arbeite heute.Tôi làm việc hôm nay.
Heute arbeite ich.Hôm nay tôi làm việc. Vietnamese keeps „tôi" before the verb; German swaps: never „Heute ich arbeite".
Ich muss heute arbeiten.Hôm nay tôi phải làm việc. The second verb goes to the end.
Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf.Tôi thức dậy lúc 7 giờ. „auf" jumps to the end.
Kommen Sie aus Vietnam?Anh / Chị đến từ Việt Nam phải không? No „không?" at the end: the verb goes first.
Verbs changeVietnamese verbs never change (tôi đi, anh đi, họ đi). German verbs take endings: ich gehe, du gehst, er geht, wir gehen. Learn these four endings first: they work for most verbs.
Sie, du, ihrInstead of ông, bà, anh, chị, em…, German has du (one friend), ihr (several friends) and Sie (polite, always with a capital S). Use Sie with everyone you would call ông, bà, anh or chị at work or at an office.
3 · Artikel & Genus · giống

der, die, das: think of them as classifiers

Vietnamese puts a classifier before the noun: cái bàn, con mèo, quyển sách. German puts an article before every noun: der Tisch, die Katze, das Buch. Just like a classifier, you must learn it with each noun. There are three: masculine, feminine and neuter, and they rarely follow logic. Nouns are always written with a capital letter.

PluralVietnamese adds các / những; German changes the noun itself: das Kind → die Kinder, der Tisch → die Tische, die Frau → die Frauen, das Auto → die Autos. Learn the plural with the noun: der Tisch, die Tische.
4 · Fälle · cách

Who does what? German shows it with the article

Vietnamese shows roles through word order alone. German also changes the article. At A1, focus on two cases: Nominativ (who does it) and Akkusativ (what or whom). Only der changes: der → den.

Your clueWhere Vietnamese uses cho (to someone), German uses the Dativ: dem, der, einem, mir, dir. Where Vietnamese uses của, German uses the Genitiv, or simply „von": das Auto von Minh = xe của Minh.

Teil 1 · Formular

Fill in the form for your friend

Ihr Freund Minh Tran ist 32 Jahre alt und kommt aus Hanoi in Vietnam. Er wohnt jetzt in der Königstraße 12, 70173 Stuttgart. Er ist Elektriker von Beruf. Er arbeitet tagsüber und möchte einen Deutschkurs am Abend machen.

Minh wants to register at the Volkshochschule. Write the 5 missing details. Bạn của bạn là Minh muốn đăng ký khóa học tiếng Đức. Hãy điền 5 thông tin còn thiếu.

Achtung!German forms use Vorname (tên) and Familienname / Nachname (họ). The postcode (PLZ) comes before the city: 70173 Stuttgart. Country: „aus Vietnam", with no article. Germans write Hanoi and Ho-Chi-Minh-Stadt, without Vietnamese marks. Dates are written day.month.year, as in Vietnam.

Teil 1 · buchstabieren

Spell your name the German way

You will be asked to spell your name. Orange letters are traps for Vietnamese speakers: C is „txê" (not „xê"), G is „ghê" (not „giê"), H is „ha" (not „hát"), J is „giót" (not „gi"), R is „e-rờ" from the throat, V is „phau" (not „vê"), W is „vê" (not „vê kép"), Y is „uyp-xi-lon" (not „i dài") and Z is „txét" (not „dét").
